How Scroll-Stitching Actually Works

Every full-page screenshot tool you have ever used is doing roughly the same trick. Here is what that trick is, where it breaks, and why the seams show up.

By the Field Notes editors · 12 min read · Updated last week

A browser cannot hand you an image of a page that does not fit on your screen. There is no API for it. The Page.captureScreenshot endpoint you may have read about in the DevTools Protocol only works for the viewport, and the deprecated captureVisibleTab extension API behaves the same way.

So tools do what you would do if you had to photocopy a newspaper column by column: scroll to a position, take a picture of what is on screen, scroll a little further, take another picture, and then paste the pictures together.

The three failure modes

Almost every ugly full-page screenshot traces back to one of three problems, and they are worth naming precisely because the fix for each is different.

  • Fixed headers repeat. A sticky navigation bar is captured in every single frame, so the finished image has the header painted down its entire length.
  • Animations drift. A lazy-loading image that finishes arriving halfway through the capture will appear in the lower frames and not the upper ones, so the same region is painted twice with different content.
  • The last frame is short. If the page is 4,050 pixels tall and the viewport is 800, the final scroll position is only 50 pixels of new content. Most implementations paste a full-height frame anyway, which stretches the tail of the page and produces the smeared, elongated look.
The tail is where the bugs live. A page whose height is not an exact multiple of the viewport height is the case that separates a tool that works from a tool that works most of the time.

Why the last frame is hard

The browser refuses to scroll past the bottom of the document. If you ask for scroll position 4,000 on a 4,050-pixel page with an 800-pixel viewport, you get 3,250 — the last legal position. The remaining 50 pixels of the page have already been visible in the previous frame, and they are visible above the new frame's top edge, not below its bottom edge.

Get this wrong and you have two options, both bad. Either you duplicate those 50 pixels, so the final image is 50 pixels too tall and the seam is visible. Or you stretch the last frame, which looks like the page was scanned on a bad printer.

Device pixel ratio is the other half

Every coordinate in the DOM is in CSS pixels. Every pixel in a captured bitmap is in device pixels. On a display with a ratio of 2, a viewport that measures 800 CSS pixels produces a bitmap that is 1,600 pixels tall, and any crop rectangle has to be scaled by that same ratio or it will be wrong by exactly a factor of two.

The failure is quiet. Nothing throws. The image comes out the right dimensions, and the top of every frame is subtly wrong.

What good looks like

  • Slice boundaries land on the viewport, and the last slice is cropped to content.
  • Sticky elements are hidden during capture and restored afterwards.
  • Scroll positions are read back from the DOM after they settle, not predicted.
  • The output is byte-for-byte identical across runs on the same page.

None of that is exotic. It is just a handful of details that have to be right, and every one of them fails silently when it is wrong — which is precisely why they deserve automated checks rather than a hopeful squint at the output.

Testing the stitch

The obvious test asserts that the output is the right size. That test passes for a completely blank image, because a blank PNG of the right dimensions is still a perfectly valid PNG of the right dimensions. The check that actually catches a broken stitch looks at the pixels: are there any non-white regions, and does every horizontal band of the output contain something?

Wrapping up

The technique is not new, but the details are where the quality lives. A tool that gets the last frame right, scales the crop by the device pixel ratio, and verifies its own output pixel by pixel will produce images that look correct every single time. One that checks the output is merely non-empty will produce a blank page roughly as often as not.
